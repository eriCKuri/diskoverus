Jet Lag Is a Light Problem, Not a Sleep Problem

Most jet lag advice focuses on sleep: nap or don't nap, take melatonin, power through. But the thing that actually resets your circadian rhythm is light, specifically when your eyes see it relative to your body's internal clock.

Traveling east means your body needs to catch up to a later time. Get outside in the morning at your destination and avoid bright light in the evening for the first two or three days. Traveling west works the opposite way: seek out afternoon and evening light, and don't fight the urge to stay up a little later than usual.

If the time difference is more than five or six hours, start shifting a day or two before you fly, moving your bedtime by 30 to 60 minutes in the direction you'll need once you land. It's a small adjustment, but it means your first real day at the destination isn't spent recovering from the flight.

June 2026 · Booking tips

The 24-Hour Rule Most Travelers Don't Know About

If you're booking a flight that departs from or arrives in the United States, and you book it at least seven days before departure, US regulations require the airline to either hold that fare without payment for 24 hours or let you cancel for a full refund within that window, no fee, no questions asked.

That's a real window to keep comparing prices, check dates against a companion's schedule, or just sleep on a big purchase without losing the fare you found. Most major airlines apply this automatically at checkout, though it's worth confirming with the specific carrier since not all of them advertise it clearly.

This protection is specific to US-linked flights. Booking within or between other countries, refund and hold policies vary a lot by airline, so it's worth reading the fare rules before you pay. Budget carriers in particular tend to charge a fee for any change, even minutes after booking.

May 2026 · Field notes

Why Shoulder Season Beats Peak Season

Every popular destination has a peak season, the weeks everyone books because the weather is reliably good and it shows up first in searches. Shoulder season is the stretch just before or after that window, when conditions are still solid but demand has dropped off.

The upside is consistent almost everywhere: fewer people at the places worth seeing, more room to book accommodation you actually want instead of whatever's left, and meaningfully lower prices on flights and hotels since airlines and properties are working to fill seats and rooms rather than turning people away. The tradeoff is usually smaller than people expect, a few degrees cooler, a slightly shorter window of daylight, maybe one or two attractions running reduced hours.

The exception is anywhere with a genuine wet or monsoon season, where the shoulder months can mean real weather risk rather than just quieter streets. For everywhere else, the two or three weeks flanking peak season are often the best version of a trip you can plan.

April 2026 · Field notes

What Nobody Tells You About Planning an African Safari

Most first-time safari planners try to cram in three or four countries and end up seeing less wildlife than someone who stayed in one region and let the animals come to them. Kenya's Maasai Mara, Tanzania's Serengeti, and Botswana's Okavango Delta each reward patience more than mileage.

Timing matters more than most guides admit. The Great Migration isn't a single event, it moves through the Serengeti and Mara on a rough annual loop, so "best time for a safari" really means "best time for the part of the migration you want to see." River crossings cluster around July through September in the Mara, while calving season runs January through March further south in the Serengeti.

Budget-wise, the biggest lever isn't the flight, it's the number of parks you visit and how far apart they are. Picking two well-matched reserves and staying four or five nights in each usually beats a rushed five-country loop, both for the wallet and for what you actually get to see.

March 2026 · Field notes

Why Southeast Asia's Best Time to Visit Depends on the Coast

Southeast Asia doesn't run on one weather calendar. Thailand, Vietnam, and the Philippines all have a west coast and an east coast, and the monsoon hits them at opposite times of year, so "rainy season in Thailand" is really only true for half the country at once.

Thailand's Andaman coast (Phuket, Krabi) gets its rain from roughly May through October, while the Gulf coast (Koh Samui, Koh Phangan) sees its wettest stretch from October through December, almost the opposite pattern. A similar split shows up across Vietnam, where the north and south run on different rhythms entirely.

The practical upshot: if a trip spans multiple regions, pack for two different weather systems, not one. There's almost always a coastline in the region having its good season while another one is having its wet one.

February 2026 · Field notes

Skip the Big Three: Why Europe's Second Cities Are Worth the Detour

Paris, Rome, and Barcelona get the visitors, but they also get the crowds, the inflated prices, and the version of a place shaped by tourism rather than by the people who actually live there. An hour or two away, the story often changes completely.

Lyon instead of Paris gets you the food culture without the queue at the Eiffel Tower. Porto instead of Lisbon trades a bit of nightlife for steep, tiled streets and river views that are arguably more photogenic. Bologna instead of Rome or Florence has the arcaded old town, the food, and a fraction of the foot traffic.

None of this means skip the famous cities entirely, they're famous for real reasons. It means building in at least one stop that isn't on everyone else's list, since that's usually where a trip stops feeling like a checklist and starts feeling like a place.

The National Park Timing Trick Most Visitors Miss

The most visited US national parks (Yosemite, Zion, Grand Teton among them) now require timed entry permits or shuttle reservations during peak months, and those slots can sell out weeks in advance. Showing up without one, especially in summer, can mean driving hours to a trailhead and getting turned away.

The trick isn't avoiding the parks in peak season, it's booking the specific permit or shuttle slot the moment reservations open, which is usually months ahead through the National Park Service's own recreation site. Many visitors don't realize the permit system exists until they're already at the gate.

Shoulder months, May and September in particular, often skip the permit requirement altogether while still offering solid weather, which makes them worth checking before automatically defaulting to July or August.

December 2025 · Field notes

Altitude Sickness Is Preventable, If You Plan for It

La Paz sits above 3,600 meters, Cusco above 3,400, and Quito above 2,800, high enough that altitude sickness is a real risk for most visitors, not just the unlucky few. Headaches, nausea, and shortness of breath in the first day or two aren't a sign something went wrong, they're the normal response to less oxygen.

The single most effective fix is also the most ignored: build in one full rest day at altitude before doing anything strenuous, and avoid flying straight into a high-altitude city and starting a trek the next morning. Hydration matters more than usual too, since the dry mountain air speeds up dehydration.

Routing helps as much as pacing. Where possible, ascend gradually rather than jumping straight to the highest point, and treat Cusco or La Paz as a place to acclimatize for a day or two before continuing on to Machu Picchu or the Uyuni salt flats.

November 2025 · Field notes

The Real Distances Behind a Quick Trip to Australia or New Zealand

Australia is roughly the same size as the continental United States, and Sydney to Perth is a longer flight than New York to Los Angeles. Trying to fit Sydney, the Great Barrier Reef, Uluru, and Perth into one week usually means spending more time in transit than at any single destination.

New Zealand looks smaller on a map, but its two islands aren't as close together as they appear, and the drive from Auckland to Queenstown is a full day even before factoring in stops. Renting a car and expecting to cover both islands in under ten days usually means driving past the scenery rather than experiencing it.

The trips that work best treat Australia and New Zealand as multiple separate destinations rather than one country to check off. Picking one region per trip tends to leave people with a much better trip than trying to see everything at once.

October 2025 · Field notes

Antarctica Isn't as Out of Reach as It Sounds

Antarctica has a reputation as the one continent ordinary travelers don't actually visit, but tens of thousands of people do it every year, almost all of them through the same route: a flight to Ushuaia, Argentina, the southernmost city in the world, followed by a two-day crossing of the Drake Passage by ship.

The season is short and fixed, running roughly November through March, Antarctica's summer, when the ice retreats enough for ships to land. Outside that window, it's genuinely not accessible to tourists at all.

Cost is the real barrier rather than logistics. Trips typically run into five figures, though last-minute bookings out of Ushuaia can be considerably cheaper for travelers with flexible dates. For anyone building an itinerary around Argentina or Chile, it's closer to reachable than most people assume.
