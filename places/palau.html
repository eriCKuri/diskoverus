Things to do in Palau

A required conservation pledge stamped directly into every visitor's passport, jellyfish that have evolved to lose their sting in an isolated marine lake, and diving consistently ranked among the very best on Earth.

Travel smart

Bottled water recommended on smaller islands. Not customary.

Palau was the first country in the world to require every incoming visitor to sign an eco-pledge, stamped directly into their passport, committing to act as a responsible environmental and cultural guest for the duration of their stay, a policy since studied and referenced by other destinations worldwide. Its Jellyfish Lake holds millions of golden jellyfish that, isolated from predators for thousands of years, have evolved to lose most of their sting, allowing visitors to snorkel directly among them in one of the most singular wildlife encounters on Earth.

When to go

Dry seasonNovember–April

The most popular window, with lower rainfall and generally better diving visibility.

Wet seasonMay–October

Warmer and more humid with regular rain, though diving conditions often remain good between showers.

Typhoon risk (lower)Generally low year-round

Palau sits at the edge of the main typhoon belt, giving it a somewhat lower direct-hit frequency than parts of the Philippines or Guam, though monitoring forecasts remains wise.

Transition periodsApril, November

Shoulder months balancing decent weather with fewer crowds between the two main seasons.

Adventure & outdoors

4

Dive the Blue Corner, one of the world's top dive sites

A dramatic wall dive with strong currents drawing large pelagic fish, sharks, and turtles, consistently ranked among the best dive sites on Earth.

adventurefullNovember–April, best visibility

Kayak among the Rock Islands' mushroom-shaped limestone formations

A UNESCO World Heritage cluster of over 300 uninhabited limestone islands, their bases eroded into distinctive mushroom shapes by tidal action.

adventurefullYear-round

Dive the Chandelier Cave near Koror

An underwater cave system with air-filled chambers holding dramatic stalactite formations, a genuinely unusual dive combining cave and reef diving.

adventurehalfYear-round

Snorkel or dive the Ulong Channel

A dramatic drift dive through a coral-lined channel, considered one of the most scenic and biodiverse dive sites in Palau's Rock Islands.

adventurefullNovember–April

Common questions

▾Do I need a visa for Palau?

Most nationalities, including US, UK, EU, Canadian, and Australian citizens, can enter visa-free for 30 days, but all visitors must sign the Palau Pledge, a conservation commitment stamped directly into their passport on arrival.

▾What is the Palau Pledge?

The world's first eco-pledge of its kind, a declaration stamped into every visitor's passport committing them to act as a responsible environmental and cultural guest, since studied and referenced by other destinations worldwide.

▾Can I really swim with jellyfish that don't sting?

Yes. Jellyfish Lake holds millions of golden jellyfish that, isolated from predators for thousands of years, have evolved to lose most of their sting, allowing safe direct snorkeling among them.

▾How many days does Palau need?

7-9 days lets you combine several days of world-class diving with a visit to Jellyfish Lake and the Rock Islands, given the country's small size but rich concentration of sites.

▾Is Palau expensive?

Yes, among the pricier Pacific destinations, reflecting its remote location, high-value low-volume tourism model, and use of the US dollar.

▾How much does it cost to visit Palau's Rock Islands?

All foreign visitors pay a Pristine Paradise Environmental Fee on arrival, currently around $100 USD, which doubles as a Rock Islands permit valid for 10 days and includes a state visitor pass. It's one of the more structured conservation-fee systems in the Pacific, reflecting Palau's strong environmental-protection stance.
