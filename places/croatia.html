Travel smart

Tap water is safe to drink. 5–10% is appreciated, not required.

Croatia's coastline includes more than a thousand islands, though only about fifty are inhabited, which is why island-hopping by ferry is less a tourist activity here than the ordinary way locals get around. The country only became independent in 1991, and its old Venetian, Austro-Hungarian and Yugoslav layers are still visible stacked on top of each other in the architecture, if you know where to look.

When to go

SpringApril–May

Plitvice's waterfalls run at full snowmelt power, and the coast warms up without the July–August crowds or prices.

Early summerJune

The Adriatic starts to feel like a proper summer sea while towns like Dubrovnik and Split are still manageable before peak season hits.

Peak summerJuly–August

The warmest water and the fullest islands — cruise ship crowds in Dubrovnik are at their heaviest, and prices follow.

AutumnSeptember–October

Warm water, thinning crowds, wine harvest in Istria and Dalmatia, and Plitvice's forests turning colour — often called the best all-round window.

Walk the boardwalks at Plitvice Lakes

Culture & history

4

Walk the walls of Dubrovnik's Old Town

Nearly two kilometres of intact medieval fortification circling the city, with Adriatic views on one side and terracotta rooftops on the other.

Explore Diocletian's Palace in Split

A Roman emperor's retirement palace from 305 AD that never stopped being lived in — it's now the core of the old city, with shops and homes built into the ancient walls.

Explore Pula's Roman amphitheatre

One of the best-preserved Roman arenas anywhere, still used for concerts and film screenings nearly two thousand years after it was built.

Visit the Museum of Broken Relationships in Zagreb

An unusual, genuinely moving collection of objects donated by strangers, each with a short story about a relationship that ended.

Food & markets

3

Truffle hunt in Istria

The Motovun forest produces both white and black truffles, and several family operations run guided hunts with trained dogs followed by a truffle lunch.

Taste wine on the Pelješac Peninsula

Home to Croatia's best-known red, Dingač, grown on near-vertical vineyards above the Adriatic near the walled town of Ston.

foodhalfYear-round, harvest Sep

Eat fresh seafood at a konoba

Small family-run taverns serving the day's catch simply grilled, alongside black risotto and Dalmatian-style peka cooked under an iron bell.

Some of the clearest water in the Mediterranean, with Vis in particular kept quieter by its distance from the mainland and years as a former military base.

Common questions

▾Is Croatia safe to visit?

Yes. Croatia is one of the more low-crime, well-organized countries in Europe to travel through, with reliable infrastructure along the coast and inland. Normal city precautions, like watching your bag in crowded old towns such as Dubrovnik and Split, are enough for most trips.

▾Do I need a visa for Croatia?

Croatia is in the Schengen area, so US, UK, and EU citizens can enter visa-free for up to 90 days within any 180-day period. Requirements vary by nationality, so check the current rules for your passport before booking.

▾How many days do you need in Croatia?

Most trips run 7 to 10 days, enough to cover Dubrovnik's Old Town, Split and Diocletian's Palace, Plitvice Lakes, and a few days island-hopping the Dalmatian coast by ferry. A shorter trip works better if you pick one region instead of trying to cover the whole coastline.

▾What's the best time to visit Croatia?

September and October are often called the best all-round window: still warm enough to swim, the July-August crowds and prices have thinned out, and it's wine harvest season in Istria and Dalmatia. June is a good early-summer alternative before peak season hits.

▾Is Croatia expensive?

It runs on the higher side of moderate. Daily costs excluding lodging typically fall between $70 and $140, and lodging runs about $30 to $85 a night. Peak summer in July and August pushes prices up considerably along the coast, so spring or fall travel stretches a budget further.

▾Do you need a car to get around Croatia?

Not necessarily. Coastal towns and the islands are well connected by ferry, and plenty of visitors island-hop without ever renting a car. A car makes more sense if you want to explore inland sights like Plitvice Lakes at your own pace.

▾How much does it cost to enter Plitvice Lakes National Park?

Peak summer (June-September) one-day adult tickets run around €40, dropping meaningfully in the shoulder and winter months. It's one of the more expensive single-park entries in the Balkans, so visiting outside the June-September peak is a genuine way to cut the cost.
